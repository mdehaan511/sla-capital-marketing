What Insurance Do Lenders Require on an Investment Property?

Published October 2026 · By the SLA Capital team

Insurance is the least glamorous item on the closing checklist and one of the most common reasons a closing slips a week. Not because the requirements are hard — because borrowers send their agent the wrong ask, the policy comes back on the wrong form or in the wrong name, and the fix eats days. Here's the whole checklist in plain terms, by loan type, so your agent gets it right the first time.

Rule one: landlord coverage, not homeowners

A homeowners (HO-3) policy insures a person living in their house. Your rental isn't that — it needs a landlord policy (often written as a dwelling-fire/DP-3 form): it covers a tenant-occupied structure, typically with loss-of-rents coverage riding along. This distinction isn't paperwork pedantry; homeowners policies routinely exclude tenant-occupied properties, which means the wrong form can mean a denied claim, not just a lender condition. Tell your agent "investment property, tenant-occupied, DP-3" and you're 80% done.

The checklist for a rental (DSCR) closing

  • Dwelling coverage the lender can accept — commonly at least the loan amount or the property's replacement cost. Underinsuring the dwelling is the #1 revision request.
  • Premises liability — standard on landlord forms; your agent will size it, and many investors add an umbrella across the portfolio as it grows.
  • Lender as mortgagee — the mortgagee clause, exactly as the lender provides it, so loss payments protect the lien.
  • The LLC as named insured — the policy must name the entity that owns the property and borrows the money (why investors close in an LLC). A policy in your personal name on an LLC-owned property is a mismatch the file can't close with — and a gap an insurer can exploit at claim time.
  • Flood insurance, if FEMA says so — properties in a special flood hazard area (zones A/V) need a separate flood policy. Not optional, not negotiable, and worth checking before you're under contract, because flood premiums change deal math.
  • Short-term rentals: running an Airbnb? Say so — STR use needs a policy or endorsement rated for it; a standard landlord form with hotel-style guests is another claim-denial setup.

Renovation and construction projects are different animals

A house mid-rehab is usually vacant, and vacancy voids most standard policies. Fix & flip projects need coverage rated for vacancy and renovation — typically a builder's risk policy or a vacant-dwelling form with renovation work disclosed — covering the structure and the work in progress, sized to the completed value rather than the as-is value. Ground-up construction is builder's risk by definition, running until certificate of occupancy. Two practical notes: make sure the policy term covers your realistic project timeline plus a buffer (extensions cost more than buying the right term up front), and when the project converts to a rental, the builder's risk policy converts to a landlord policy at the refinance — same checklist as above.

How to never lose a week on insurance

Order it early — the day you're under contract, not the week of closing. Send your agent one message: property address, "investment purchase closing [date]," entity name exactly as registered, loan amount, the lender's mortgagee clause, and whether the property is tenant-occupied, vacant-for-rehab, or ground-up. Everything in this article compresses into that one email, and a competent agent returns an acceptable binder in a day or two. At SLA the insurance review happens as soon as your binder hits the file — on a 72-hour close, the borrowers who hit that timeline are the ones whose agent got the full ask on day one.
