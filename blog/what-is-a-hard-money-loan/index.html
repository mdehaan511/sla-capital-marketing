What Is a Hard Money Loan and How Does It Work?

Published October 2026 · By the SLA Capital team

A hard money loan is a short-term real estate loan made by a private lender and secured by the property itself — the "hard" asset — instead of your pay stubs. It funds in days, costs more per month than a bank, and exists for exactly one reason: some deals are won or lost on speed. Here's how the loans actually work, what they really cost, and the honest test for whether hard money is your right tool or just your expensive one.

The core trade: asset-based speed

A bank underwrites you — income, tax returns, DTI — which takes weeks. A hard money lender underwrites the deal: what the property is worth today, what it's worth fixed up, your plan, and your skin in the game. That's why a hard money loan can go from application to funded in days (here's the 72-hour anatomy), while the same purchase through a bank dies in processing. You're not paying higher interest for nothing — you're buying certainty and speed, the two things sellers of good deals actually care about.

The anatomy of the loan

  • Term: 6–24 months. This is transitional money with a built-in deadline, not hold-forever financing.
  • Payment: interest-only monthly, principal due at exit (sale or refinance). On better loans, interest accrues only on the drawn balance during a rehab.
  • Sizing: a percent of total project cost (purchase + rehab), capped against the after-repair value — the full logic is in our ARV guide. Experienced borrowers reach up to 92.5% loan-to-cost, with 100% of the rehab budget funded through draws.
  • Cost: roughly 9.5%–12% interest and 1–4 points, depending on experience, leverage, and the deal (current published rates).
  • Vesting: business-purpose loans to an LLC, with a personal guaranty. Not for buying a home you'll live in.

Hard money vs. bridge vs. fix-and-flip: same animal

The industry uses the names loosely, and mostly they're the same product. "Hard money" is the umbrella term. "Bridge loan" emphasizes the purpose — spanning the gap between buying and your exit. "Fix-and-flip loan" emphasizes the rehab budget riding along in draws. When a lender quotes you any of the three, the questions that matter are identical: rate, points, term, draw mechanics, and what happens at maturity.

What it really costs — and why the math still works

Take a $200K purchase with a $50K rehab, financed six months at 10.5% interest-only: carry runs roughly $9,000–$11,000, plus points at closing. Against a bank that's expensive. Against the deal, it's a line item: if the speed got you a property $30K under market because you could close in a week, the financing cost was the cheapest thing in the project. That's the honest frame — hard money is priced per month, so it rewards operators who budget properly and execute fast, and punishes projects that drift.

The exit is the whole ballgame

Every hard money loan ends one of two ways: you sell (the flip) or you refinance into long-term debt (the hold). Lenders underwrite the exit before funding, and so should you — the deal must pencil both ways before you buy. If the plan is to hold, the exit is a DSCR loan qualified on the property's rent, and with no seasoning period it can close as soon as the stabilized value is defensible (the step-by-step exit). The investors who get hurt by hard money are almost never hurt by the rate — they're hurt by reaching maturity without an exit ready.

When hard money is the right tool

Use it when the deal needs speed or work: auctions, estate sales, properties too rough for bank financing, value-add projects, or any purchase where a 7-day close wins the contract. Skip it when a stabilized rental and a patient seller let long-term DSCR financing do the job from day one — the comparison is in Hard Money vs. DSCR.
